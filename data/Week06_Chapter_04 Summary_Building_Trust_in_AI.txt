Week 6 - Chapter 4 Summary: Building Trust in AI

This chapter focuses on one of the most important topics in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) and AI design:

People will only use AI effectively if they trust it.

The chapter explains how designers can build, maintain, and calibrate trust between users and AI systems through explainability, transparency, user control, and thoughtful interface design.

Key Concept: Trust in AI

Trust is the willingness to take a risk because you expect a beneficial outcome. Just as people trust doctors, banks, or businesses, users must trust AI systems before they rely on them. AI systems are not perfect because they are probabilistic and sometimes make mistakes. Therefore, designers must create experiences that help users understand when to trust AI and when to use their own judgment.

The chapter identifies four major components of trust:

1. Competence

The AI must successfully perform its intended task and provide meaningful value.

Example:

Google Search generally returns useful information.
2. Reliability

The AI should perform consistently over time.

Example:

iPhone features work predictably and consistently.
3. Predictability

Users should understand how the system behaves.

Example:

Spreadsheet software should not unexpectedly change common functions.
4. Benevolence

Users must believe the system is acting in their best interest.

Example:

Patagonia encourages customers to repair products instead of buying new ones.
Trust Calibration

Users can develop either:

Overtrust

Trust exceeds the AI's actual capability.

Example:

Blindly following stock market predictions from an AI.
Distrust

Trust is lower than the AI's capability.

Example:

Ignoring accurate recommendations because users do not believe the system.

The goal of AI design is proper trust calibration, where user trust matches the system's actual capabilities.

Two Foundations of Trustworthy AI

The chapter emphasizes two essential design principles:

1. Explainability

Users should understand:

What the AI does
How it works
Why it made a recommendation
How accurate it is

Explainability helps users build accurate mental models and trust the system appropriately.

Guidelines for Good Explanations
Make clear what the system can do
Explain how well it performs
Set expectations for adaptation
Be transparent
Build cause-and-effect relationships
Optimize explanations for understanding rather than technical detail
2. User Control

Users should never be forced to blindly trust AI.

They should be able to:

Edit information
Correct mistakes
Ignore recommendations
Choose among alternatives
Provide feedback
Opt out of features

Giving users control increases trust and encourages long-term adoption.

Types of AI Explanations

The chapter introduces several explanation methods.

Data Use Explanations

Explain:

What data is collected
How data is used
Privacy implications

Example:

A navigation app explaining that it used calendar appointments, GPS location, and traffic information to recommend a departure time.

Description-Based Explanations

Simple explanations of why a recommendation was made.

Example:

"This recipe was recommended because you like broccoli, mushrooms, and pasta."

These are often more useful than detailed technical explanations.

Confidence-Based Explanations

Show how confident the AI is.

Examples:

High confidence
Medium confidence
Low confidence

Instead of:

87.4% confidence

Use:

"We're fairly confident about this recommendation."

This is often easier for users to understand.

Experimentation-Based Explanations

Allow users to learn by trying the system.

Examples:

Demo environments
Sandboxes
Interactive onboarding

Users develop trust by observing the AI's behavior directly.

Evaluating AI Explanations

The chapter recommends evaluating explanations through:

Qualitative Methods
User interviews
Surveys
Observation
Task completion testing
Quantitative Methods
Analytics
Product logs
Usage metrics

The authors note that qualitative feedback is usually more useful for understanding trust and explainability issues.

User Control Mechanisms

Two major categories are discussed.

Data Control

Users should be able to:

View their data
Edit their data
Delete data
Reset preferences
Opt out

Transparency and privacy are critical trust-building factors.

Control Over AI Output

Users should be able to:

Choose recommendations
Ignore recommendations
Dismiss recommendations
Correct mistakes
Provide feedback

Examples include:

Netflix thumbs up/down
YouTube recommendation feedback
Spotify song preferences
Opportunities to Build Trust

Trust should be reinforced throughout the user journey.

Onboarding

Explain:

What the AI does
What data it uses
How it learns
User Interactions

Reinforce expectations and explain recommendations.

Updates

Communicate changes to:

Features
Privacy policies
Algorithms
Settings

Provide transparency and control.

Error States

Use mistakes as opportunities to explain, educate, and rebuild trust.

Personality and Emotion in AI

The chapter discusses whether AI should have personalities.

Examples:

Siri
Alexa
Google Assistant

Potential benefits:

More engaging interactions
Better collaboration
Improved user comfort

Potential risks:

Users overtrust the AI
Users reveal sensitive information
Unrealistic expectations
Emotional attachment
Manipulation concerns
Guidelines for AI Personality Design

If personality is used:

Do not pretend to be human
Clearly communicate limitations
Consider cultural differences
Be inclusive
Use appropriate tone of voice
Avoid reinforcing stereotypes

The chapter repeatedly emphasizes:

Users should always know they are interacting with AI.
